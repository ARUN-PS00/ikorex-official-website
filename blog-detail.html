The Smallest Tasks Can Become Your Biggest Operational Cost

A task that takes 15 or 20 minutes rarely feels like a business problem. But repeated across days, employees, and systems, the numbers change dramatically.

Yesudas Sebastian Co-Founder & CEO, iKOREX
Published: January 15, 2025 • Reading Time: 6 min read • Operational ROI Guide

Figure 1.0: Repetitive manual tasks quietly consume thousands of hours across organizations when multiplied over 260 working days.

A task that takes 15 or 20 minutes rarely feels like a business problem.

It feels too small to worry about. You might think, "It's only 20 minutes. We'll just get it done." Once, that may be true.

But when the same task is repeated every working day, by multiple employees, throughout the year, the numbers change. A 20-minute task repeated across 260 working days equals 5,200 minutes — approximately 87 hours, or around 11 working days, for one person. If five people perform the same task, that becomes approximately 435 hours, or 55 working days of labour.

The task didn't become more complicated. It simply happened repeatedly. That is where many businesses underestimate the true cost of manual work.

The Reality of Repetitive Overhead
The real cost of repetitive work isn't measured in minutes. It's measured in what those minutes become when repeated across your business. Daily cost can look small; annual cost can be significant.

1. The Problem Isn't the 20 Minutes

When businesses estimate a task, they often measure only the obvious activity: "It takes 15 minutes to update this report."

But what actually happens? Someone opens a file, finds the information, switches between systems, checks the previous entry, identifies an error, corrects it, updates the record and sends the result.

The task isn't really just "15 minutes of updating." It is a sequence of small actions: finding, switching, checking, correcting, updating and sending.

Businesses often count the doing while overlooking the finding, switching, checking and correcting around it. When those actions happen every day, the hidden cost starts to grow.

2. The Work Nobody Puts on the Schedule

Businesses schedule meetings, sales calls, projects and customer work. But repetitive operational work often happens in the background.

Employees may spend part of every day entering data, updating spreadsheets, processing invoices, preparing reports, checking records, copying information between systems, updating customer details, sending routine emails and processing approvals.

None of these tasks necessarily looks significant on its own. But they consume something every business has a limited amount of: human time.

The important question is not only, "How long does this task take?" It is:

"How much time does this process consume across the entire organisation?"

3. Small Time Losses Multiply Quickly

One employee spending 20 minutes every working day on the same manual process uses approximately 87 hours a year. For five employees, that becomes approximately 435 hours, or 55 working days of labour.

And that calculation considers only the time spent performing the task. It does not include errors, rework, delays, interruptions or the value of the work employees could have completed instead.

4. The Hidden Cost of Manual Work

Manual work creates far more than a simple time cost. When evaluated end-to-end, it introduces five compounding liabilities:

1. Labour Cost
Someone has to perform the task repeatedly, tying up salaried staff on administrative routines.
2. Context Switching
Employees constantly move between email, spreadsheets, accounting platforms and CRMs to complete one workflow.
3. Human Error
Repetitive work creates continuous opportunities for incorrect entries, missed records and outdated data.
4. Cost of Rework
When something goes wrong, someone has to investigate, correct, recheck and communicate the change across teams.
5. The Opportunity Cost
The employee completing repetitive administration could instead be following up with a customer, analysing business performance, improving customer experience, or working on strategic growth projects. This means the real cost is not simply the time spent doing the task—it is also the higher-value work that did not happen during that time.

5. Not Every Repetitive Task Needs Automation

The answer isn't to automate everything simply because it is repetitive. Strong automation candidates generally share four distinct characteristics:

  • They are repetitive: The same activity happens again and again in the same pattern.
  • They are rule-based: The process follows defined steps, conditions, or logic.
  • They are frequent: The task happens daily, weekly, or at high volume.
  • They are easy to underestimate: The individual activity looks small, but the cumulative impact across the workforce is significant.

These characteristics provide a practical starting point for identifying automation opportunities. The goal isn't to automate because automation is available. The goal is to automate where it makes clear business sense.

6. Start With the Process, Not the Technology

A common mistake is starting the automation conversation with technology: "Where can we use AI?"

A far better question is: "Where are we repeatedly spending human time?"

Start with one workflow and document every single step. For example, a routine invoice or record workflow:

Receive Information → Open Document → Extract Data → Check Information → Enter Data → Verify → Send Result

Once you lay out the sequence, ask the diagnostic questions:

  • Which steps are repetitive?
  • Which follow fixed rules?
  • Which require human judgement?
  • How frequently does the process occur?
  • How many people perform it?
  • How much time does it consume?
  • What happens when something goes wrong?

This gives you a clear, objective picture of where automation will deliver tangible value.

7. Calculate the Cost Before You Automate

Before implementing any software bot or AI tool, measure the current process. You don't need complex modeling software; use this straightforward equation:

Annual Manual Cost Formula
Annual Manual Time = Time per Task × Tasks per Day × Working Days × Employees
Example: 20 minutes × 1 task × 260 working days × 5 employees = 26,000 minutes.
That is approximately 433 hours, or more than 54 eight-hour working days.

This calculation does not automatically mean the process should be automated. It makes the cost visible. Once the cost is visible, leadership can make an informed, data-driven decision about whether the workflow deserves immediate attention.

8. Don't Automate a Broken Process

Automation should not simply make an inefficient process faster. Automating bad steps merely produces errors at machine speed. Follow this sequence:

  • First: Understand the workflow end-to-end.
  • Then: Identify and eliminate unnecessary steps.
  • Then: Standardise the process logic and validation rules.
  • Then: Determine which parts should be automated.

The objective must always be to create a better workflow, not just a faster version of the old one.

9. Start With One Process

You don't need to automate your entire business at once. Start with one repetitive process. Measure it. Understand it. Calculate its cost. Then evaluate whether automation can improve it.

Ask:

  • How often does it happen?
  • How many people perform it?
  • How much time does it consume?
  • How many systems are involved?
  • How often does rework occur?
  • What does that time cost the business?

Then ask the fundamental question: "Does this process really need a person to perform every step?" If the answer is no, you have found an automation opportunity.

10. The Most Valuable Process Might Be the Most Boring One

Businesses often look for complex, glamorous processes when considering automation. But some of the most profitable opportunities are found in ordinary, everyday workflows:

  • The report someone prepares every morning.
  • The spreadsheet someone updates every afternoon.
  • The invoices someone processes every week.
  • The customer information someone enters into multiple systems.
  • The approval workflow that follows the exact same steps every time.

These processes don't look impressive. But they are frequent, repetitive, rule-based and easy to underestimate. That is exactly why they deserve attention.

11. Automation Is About More Than Saving Hours

Business automation is not simply about making employees work faster. It is about improving how work moves through the organisation.

A well-designed automated workflow can help reduce repetitive manual work, processing time, human error, administrative effort, rework and operational bottlenecks. It gives employees more time for activities that require human judgement, communication, problem-solving and decision-making.
